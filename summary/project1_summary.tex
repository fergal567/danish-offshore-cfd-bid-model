{\bfseries\Large Reverse-engineering Vattenfall's 2026 Danish offshore wind bids}\par
\vspace{2pt}
{\itshape What do DKK 504/MWh (Nordsøen Midt) and DKK 542/MWh (Hesselø) imply about cost, return and wind?}\par
\vspace{3pt}
{\small\color{grey} Fergal Israel, MSc Wind Energy, Technical University of Denmark (DTU) \quad October 2026}\par

\vspace{4pt}
\rule{\linewidth}{0.8pt}\par\vspace{4pt}
{\fontsize{11}{14}\selectfont Vattenfall's winning prices are 3--6\,\% below what a public-data model needs to break even. They work at a real after-tax return of about 4.5--4.7\,\%, about 1\,pp above Vattenfall's own discount rate for wind, or if it builds 4--6\,\% cheaper than my estimate from Danish Energy Agency (DEA) data. With 15\,MW turbine costs the return falls below that rate: the bids look like a bet on 20\,MW turbines. Nordsøen Midt carries more downside: its cap on state payments can run out if power prices are low.\par}
\vspace{4pt}\rule{\linewidth}{0.8pt}\par

\head{What it means for the next tenders}
\footnotesize
Return requirements and turbine costs decided these bids more than wind did. Nordsøen Syd (bids due October 2028) has a DKK 6.6bn cap, under half of Nordsøen Midt's (about DKK 75/MWh of top-up), so its bidders must price low-price risk explicitly. {\color{grey}(My interpretation.)}
